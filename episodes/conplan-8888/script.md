## COLD OPEN — DAY 0 · 2:06 A.M.

*[SFX: three harsh emergency-alert bursts, phones everywhere at once]*

Two-oh-six a.m. Every phone on the highway screams at the same time.

*Emergency alert. Shelter in place. Do not approach the injured. Do not travel.*

You're already travelling, because your sister warned you first.

Jo is a nurse in the medical wing at Calder Air Station. Just after one, the guards carried in three bitten drivers from the interstate. By one-thirty, two had stopped breathing. Then they started moving.

The doors sealed. Phones inside were collected. Before they took hers, Jo sent you four words.

*North gate. Come now.*

So you came.

You crest the last hill, and there it is. Floodlights. Concrete barriers. A fence. And in front of the north gate, two hundred cars. Every one of them honking.

The gate is closed.

A loudspeaker on the guard tower repeats one sentence, every thirty seconds.

**LOUDSPEAKER (male, flat):** *This installation is closed.*

## THE REAL PLAN

Here's the part that's real.

In 2011, officers training at U.S. Strategic Command wrote a plan for a zombie apocalypse. CONPLAN 8888. **`[REAL · p.1]`** It was a classroom exercise, chosen because no one could ever mistake it for a real threat.

But the paper is real. And so are its orders. One table says: lock down for thirty days. **`[REAL · p.9]`** The written orders go further. *Shelter all mission essential personnel in place for at least forty days.* **`[REAL · p.12]`**

Jo is mission essential.

You are not.

The real plan says nothing about people waiting at a gate. That part is our story. And in our story, this gate is not going to open.

---

## DECISION ONE — THE FENCE

Listen to the horns. Every one is a dinner bell.

The plan's authors guessed how the dead would move. They wrote that zombies cannot drive, climb or swim, and that they would follow the roads. **`[REAL · p.25]`** Behind you is the interstate.

Stay with the crowd and the light? Or walk into the dark?

You walk.

A mile east, black against the sky, there's an old grain elevator. Its steel ladder starts twelve feet up, so kids can't climb it. From the top, you'd see the whole base.

Halfway across the field, you hear a boy, maybe ten, in a red hoodie, standing between the cars, saying one word over and over.

*Dad.*

His name is Eli. His father walked to the gate an hour ago to argue. He hasn't come back.

You don't decide. Your hand just takes his.

Behind you, the horns begin to stop. One by one.

---

## THE CLIMB

At the foot of the elevator, your flashlight finds an aluminum extension ladder lying in the weeds.

You raise it. It just reaches the first steel rung. Eli climbs first. Then, from the narrow landing, you haul the ladder up after you and lay it along the rail.

Now there is nothing below you but twelve feet of smooth concrete.

If the planners guessed right, nothing dead can follow you up.

---

## DAY 1 — THE TOWER

Dawn. You count what you have. One bottle of water. A granola bar Eli is pretending not to watch. A pocketknife. A phone with no signal and thirty-one percent.

From up here, the base looks peaceful. Somewhere in there, Jo is safe.

Down in the field, hundreds have gathered along the fence.

Emergency guidance says a gallon of water per person, per day, at least half of it just to drink. **`[SURVIVAL FACT]`** There are two of you. You have one bottle.

---

## DAY 3 — RAIN

The planners wrote that rain will be vitally important, and that streams and rivers will be unreliable. **`[REAL · p.8]`**

On day three, it rains.

You stretch your jacket across the walkway and funnel every drop into the bottle, into the wrapper, into your cupped hands. Eli laughs for the first time.

Remember that laugh. You'll need it.

---

## DAY 6 — THE NOON RUN

One storm won't last forty days. You need something to catch rain and store it. There's a farmhouse half a mile north.

The plan made one more guess: the infected kind might be less active when the sun is bright. **`[REAL · p.8]`**

A guess. Written in a classroom.

At noon, under a hard white sun, you lower the ladder and climb down.

One rule. If you're not back when the tower's shadow touches the fence, Eli pushes the ladder into the wheat. Then nobody comes up. Not even you.

The farmhouse gives you a blue tarp, two five-gallon buckets, food, a first-aid kit, a hand-crank emergency radio, and the thing that matters most: a jug of plain, unscented bleach.

On the way back, one of them is standing in the tower's shade. Just standing. Facing the base.

You lie in the wheat until a cloud covers the sun and it drifts away.

At the top, Eli has both hands on the ladder, ready to push. He'd started counting.

That night the tarp becomes a funnel over a bucket, and you teach him the rule that keeps you alive. Eight drops of bleach per gallon of clear water. Stir. Wait thirty minutes. Then drink. **`[SURVIVAL FACT]`**

---

## DAY 12 — THE LIVING

On the twelfth night: headlights.

A pickup stops at the foot of the tower. Three men who saw what you saw: the one high place for miles.

The ladder is still down. You forgot.

You haul it up rung over rung as a flashlight finds it, and fingers close on empty air.

They shout for an hour. Then the noise brings the dead out of the dark, and the truck is gone, and you are both still breathing, very quietly, in the black.

The dead were never the only thing outside the fence.

---

## DAY 18 — THE LIGHT

Every night at nine, you flash your phone toward the base. Three short. Three long. Three short.

On night eighteen, a window in the medical wing flashes back.

Three times. And every night after that.

You tell Eli it's your sister. You don't know that. But it's the best thing you have.

---

## DAY 24 — THE CREEK

Then the rain stops. Six dry days. The buckets go down an inch a day, then two, then nothing.

The plan warns that the dead will be drawn to drinking water, because that's where the living have to go. **`[REAL · p.26]`**

The only water left is the creek behind the farmhouse.

You go at noon. You fill both buckets with brown, cloudy water. And Eli, who promised to stay, who followed you anyway, slips in the mud.

A hand comes out of the reeds.

You pull him so hard you both fall backward into the wheat. You run. You climb. You don't stop shaking until dark.

There's a long red scratch on Eli's forearm.

*A branch,* he says. *It was a branch.*

That night you let the water settle and pour it through a T-shirt before the bleach, because cloudy water has to be filtered first. **`[SURVIVAL FACT]`** You do every step right. It doesn't make you feel safe.

---

## DAY 27 — FEVER

The plan has a line about this, too. It warns that the newly infected may deny what's happening to them. **`[REAL · p.15]`**

On day twenty-seven, Eli has a fever.

Not high. He eats. He talks. He sleeps against your shoulder with the red sleeve pulled down over his arm, while you sit awake doing the planners' math.

They assumed the infected wouldn't last past forty days. **`[REAL · p.30]`** That's why their whole strategy is to wait.

Day thirty-one, the rain returns. Day thirty-five, the fever comes and goes. The scratch doesn't heal.

And on page thirty, about people who can't keep up, the real plan says plainly: *All who fall behind must be left behind.* **`[REAL · p.30]`**

It was written for a training exercise.

You are not in a training exercise.

---

## DAY 40 — THE BROADCAST

Day forty. Before dawn, you touch Eli's forehead, and it's burning. His shirt is soaked. When you ease the red sleeve back, the scratch has swollen, angry and red at the edges. Whatever it is, anyone at that gate will see it in a second.

The real plan has a detail almost no one talks about. After at least forty days, patrols go out, and every report is broadcast in plain language, on open channels, so survivors can find them. **`[REAL · p.12]`**

At dawn, the hand-crank radio wakes up.

A woman's voice. Tired. Steady. Your sister's voice.

**JO (radio):** *To any survivors north of the installation. We can take two. Walk to the north gate at noon. No injuries. No fever. We will check.*

A pause.

**JO (radio):** *I'm sorry. Those are the rules.*

Eli is awake. He heard every word. He looks down at his sleeve. Then at you.

**ELI (whispered, narrator's voice):** *Say I'm fine. Please. Say I'm fine.*

Tell the truth, and they'll take you, and only you. He stays on this tower alone, with a fever and a ladder he can't lift.

Lie, and walk him to that gate, and you're betting your sister's whole building on a scratch you can't explain.

Your thumb is on the button.

And below you, at the foot of the tower, something knocks against the steel.

Your ladder is still up here.

Someone has brought their own.

The plan says they can't climb.

So who's coming up?
